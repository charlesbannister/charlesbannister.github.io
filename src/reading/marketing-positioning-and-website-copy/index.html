The argument I’d build the website around

Keep the line you chose. “I help marketing teams figure out where software and AI make a real difference.” It sounds like you, and it leaves room for a five-minute fix as well as a substantial piece of software. The sentence underneath has a harder job. It has to help someone recognise a moment from their own working week.

Sometimes a client says the phone’s gone quiet. Sometimes a sale starts on Friday and the ads still have last month’s offer in them. I build tools that help you work out what needs attention, check the details and do something about it.

That’s a line to test. The two examples do different work. The quiet phone shows your judgement. There could be several explanations and the right next step might be a call to the sales team. The outdated offer shows where a repeatable check earns its keep. A buyer who recognises either situation has a reason to keep reading.

The common thread is what happens after someone buys. With Shabba, a marketer leaves with a setup they can use when the next question arrives. With custom work, a business gets a job done the way its people need it done. Your Google Ads experience makes both offers credible. Your ability to write, connect and operate the software makes them possible.

The first part of this document is for thinking and marking up. The last part collects the current words from all three mock-up pages in reading order. Draft alternatives appear in the strategy notes so you can try them against the current copy.

Four books, four decisions about this business

Marketing Management · Which buyer gets the first paragraph?

Philip Kotler, Kevin Lane Keller and Alexander Chernev give you the broad sequence. Segment the market into groups with meaningfully different needs. Decide which group to serve first. Then shape an offer and a position that fit that group. The choice affects price, delivery and proof as much as wording. A segment earns its own message when membership changes why or how someone buys. Pearson’s chapter outline, especially chapters 6 and 7

My first buyer for Shabba: an in-house marketer who already works in Google Ads, uses a Mac, can get access to the relevant accounts, and regularly has to explain what changed. This is a hypothesis drawn from our discussion. It’s narrower than “marketing teams” in a useful way. It tells us whom to show in the video, what they bring to the call and what a successful session looks like. They should be able to repeat a check on their own machine.

An agency lead may buy exactly the same session for a different reason. The lead wants a colleague to handle recurring checks consistently and come back with something worth a decision. That gives you a sentence for the product page: “Bring the person who’ll actually use it. We’ll set it up on their Mac, and the rest of the team can join us.” Keep freelancers welcome. We have no buyer evidence that their budgets or technical ability rule them out.

A decision for the custom page: qualify by situation. A business with a changing feed, an expensive delay and a clear rule can be a better prospect than a bigger company with a vague interest in AI. Ask what happens between a fact changing and somebody acting on it. That’s where the ZIP code project earned its place.

Value Proposition Design · What makes a job worth paying to improve?

Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda and Smith separate the customer’s jobs, pains and desired gains from the product you could supply. First understand and rank what matters to a particular buyer. Then map each part of the offer to that profile and test the match. A pain can be social or emotional as well as financial. Frequency, severity and the buyer’s own definition of success help you decide what deserves attention. Strategyzer’s guide to the Value Proposition Canvas

Take “the phone’s a little bit too quiet.” The marketer’s practical job is to find out whether something has gone wrong. Their professional job is to give a client or manager a credible answer. The hardest part might be knowing whether to intervene at all. If enquiries have halved and the remaining enquiries are excellent, a hurried campaign edit could make things worse. Useful software helps someone compare like periods, identify which leads went missing and see where the evidence ends.

Now map that back to the offer. Connected data reduces the work of gathering evidence. The audit exposes a setting and the standard it was checked against. Chat lets the buyer ask a follow-up. The review queue gives them a point at which to decide. Each feature has a reason to exist in the story. The question “Can you find the reason sales fell?” belongs in a longer investigation. In two hours, the deliverable you control is a working setup, plus examples if time allows.

A second value story is accuracy. Your invoice example makes this unusually clear. The PDF opens beside a checklist. Is it the right month, the right amount, the right recipient, and is the invoice attached? Checking still takes time. The value is avoiding a correction after the client has received it. “Sometimes it means making a job easier to get right” deserves to stay.

Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind · What will the buyer remember?

Al Ries and Jack Trout start with the associations already in a prospect’s mind. A familiar category gives someone a quick way to understand a new offer. It also brings expectations. Calling the session “coaching” suggests advice. Calling it “desktop software with guided setup” tells the buyer to expect an installed product and a working connection. The same two hours sound quite different. McGraw Hill’s overview

Your name and Shabba need different jobs. Charles is the person someone recommends when a marketing team wants to put software to work. Shabba Desktop is a specific thing they can acquire. That explains the three-page structure. The homepage introduces you and gives two routes. The product page answers “What do I get?” The custom page answers “Could you build something for the way we work?”

A practical test: show the homepage to an in-house marketer for half a minute. Close it. Ask how they would describe you to their manager and what they remember being able to buy. If they remember “he manages Google Ads,” your proof has swallowed your positioning. If they remember “AI consultant” but can’t name anything they could take away, the offer needs a more concrete example.

Obviously Awesome · What would the buyer do without you?

April Dunford works from a buyer’s real alternatives through the capabilities that differ, the value those differences create, the people who care most and the category that makes that value easy to see. Starting with alternatives disciplines the exercise. A capability only helps your case if the buyer would miss it in the option they would otherwise choose. Dunford’s quick-start guide

If the alternative is doing it manually, show the second run. The first run is a demonstration. The second, driven by the customer, shows they can do next week’s review themselves. If the alternative is building with AI, take that seriously. A capable marketer might assemble something good. Your case is that they can start with twelve working tools, adjustable standards and your help getting connected. Ask what they’d enjoy building and what they’d gladly skip.

If the alternative is an existing PPC platform, ask what the buyer already uses and what gap remains. A team happy with its platform might still pay for one custom connection. A comparison based purely on monthly fees misses that opportunity. If the alternative is leaving it alone, find the cost of waiting. “How many clicks could you buy between a buyer dropping out and someone updating the targeting?” is a better conversation than a general promise of efficiency.

My strongest positioning hypothesis is a working starting point for people who already know their marketing job and want to do more with their own data. The best way to challenge it is to ask recent users what they had actually tried before speaking to you. Read Obviously Awesome first for this question. Use Value Proposition Design alongside the interviews, Marketing Management for the audience choice, and Ries and Trout when you edit the headline.

What the research changes about the pitch

Your November r/PPC research found repeated concerns about routine manual reviews, generic AI advice, mistakes going unnoticed, difficult setup and changes going live without enough oversight. It’s an AI-assisted analysis of a keyword-filtered community sample. Treat it as a source of interview questions. Someone posting about a problem has given you a clue about demand. A paid booking and a repeat use provide stronger evidence. Local r/PPC research report

There’s a good reason to demonstrate the check behind an AI answer. In a field experiment with consultants, AI assistance improved performance on some tasks and made correct answers less likely on a difficult task outside its capabilities. That study concerns consulting work, so I wouldn’t transfer its percentages to Google Ads. The practical lesson for your video is to show one place where the marketer can inspect a finding and exercise judgement. Dell’Acqua and colleagues, Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier

The paid-and-organic angle also benefits from precision. Google already documents reporting that compares paid and organic search appearances. The interesting demonstration is what a practitioner does with the comparison. Take one valuable paid query, identify the page appearing organically, and decide whether the SEO colleague has a specific opportunity to investigate. Compare equivalent dates and markets. Search Console can return only its top rows, so a missing query is a lead to investigate. Google’s paid and organic report · Search Console query documentation

A bank of angles, in the order I’d try them

1. Leave with something working. Lead the product page with this. “Bring the Mac you use for work. We’ll connect your account together, run a check and make sure you can do it again when I’m off the call.” Proof is a buyer operating the setup themselves. A wall of logos can come afterwards.

2. Ask a question that takes more than one tab. Use this to make the possibilities exciting. “The date’s in Calendar. The offer’s in Gmail. The products are in Sheets. Do the ads match what we’re actually selling on Friday?” Show one mismatch in demo data. That’s more memorable than naming four integrations.

3. Spend where the business can earn money. Lead the custom-work proof with the ZIP code case. When the last buyer stopped taking calls in an area, a click could cost the business money even if the ad did its job perfectly. The feed and the team’s rules gave the software a reason to change the targeting. This supports a claim about removing a specific source of waste. Any claim about total money saved would need client figures. Campaign Zip Sync

4. Give an experienced marketer a useful second pair of eyes. “Which live ads still mention an offer that ended last week?” Show the exact text and a real expiry date. An agency lead can immediately picture a colleague reviewing the result. An in-house marketer can picture catching the issue before a customer does.

5. Find work worth doing. Start with organic queries for a PPC test, or converting paid queries for an SEO investigation. For ecommerce, follow one product from Merchant Center status into the relevant campaign. Let the viewer see how a question becomes a shortlist. Describe this as a way to investigate rather than promising an automatic conclusion.

6. Decide when to leave an account alone. “Calls dipped last week. Does that normally happen around this holiday?” Check an equivalent period and ask about lead quality before proposing changes. This angle demonstrates your analytical judgement. Automated seasonality detection was an idea in our discussion. Use the reasoning in an article now and demonstrate automation only once there’s a working example.

7. Put the tool where the job happens. A scheduled check posts broken URLs in Slack. A colleague fixes a page and clicks to run the check again. This is a possible custom build. The first run saves someone remembering the check. The second gives them a quick way to confirm the fix. Hosting, schedules, logs and alerts belong here because the business needs the job to run again tomorrow.

8. Let the right person supply the answer. You wrote the ads. The client knew which wording sounded right. A shared editing page gave them an easy way to make the change and hand it back. This gives you a client-communication story grounded in your own experience. The invoice checklist and a website quote calculator can sit alongside it as examples of modest tools with clear owners.

“What if I already use Claude or Codex?” That’s a good starting point. Show how to ask a question there with Shabba running in the background. Closing the Mac window is fine. The first video, and what to learn from it

Record one short preview of the outcome of the session. Open on a marketer asking which campaign settings differ from their standards. Show one finding next to the standard. Let the viewer see why a deviation might be deliberate. Queue a suggested edit and pause at review. Close the Shabba window and repeat a supported query from the external assistant. End on what the buyer needs to bring and what they can do afterwards. Use a demo account and a repeatable result. Verify the whole sequence before promising it on the page.

A second video could show the paid-to-organic investigation. A third could answer “Are we ready for Friday’s sale?” using the connected services. These are good follow-ups because each starts with a recognisable decision. The viewer can judge whether they’d use the workflow next week.

For the first few paid sessions, keep a short record of four things: what prompted the booking, what the buyer had already tried, which task they ran themselves, and what they used again the following week. Ask, “If you hadn’t booked, what would you have done?” and “What would you tell a colleague you bought?” Their answers will tell you which of the four books’ ideas is earning its keep.

Read the evidence in order. If people remember the offer but don’t enquire, ask about access, price and timing. If they book and only use one tool, find out why that tool matters. It could give you your best demonstration. If they come for setup and consistently ask for bespoke work, keep the product offer intact and give those buyers a clearer route to the custom page. A small number of interviews can shape better wording. A run of paid sessions and repeat use can justify a larger change.
