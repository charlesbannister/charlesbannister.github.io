Content notes: five Google Ads free tools

Points you could cover in a piece on each of the five new free tools: the settings audit, the broken link checker, the Quality Score dashboard, account breakdowns and page speed. Each piece starts broad, says why the subject matters, what it covers and how to act on it, and only then mentions the tool and how readers could build their own.

How to read these

Most points come from the Google Ads knowledge base (the corpus behind the k chat). I asked it about each subject separately, with about 70 searches in all, and only kept what it could back up. Each point carries a tag:

  • Google Google's own documented behaviour, from the knowledge base.
  • Practitioner practitioner advice from the knowledge base: a common approach, not a rule.
  • Outside not in the knowledge base. Added from Google's public documentation (mostly the Google Ads API and PageSpeed Insights). Worth checking against the current help pages before you say it on camera.

The knowledge base is thin in places. It has only four entries on Quality Score itself, and nothing on Core Web Vitals, the Destination not working policy, ad schedules or bid adjustments. Those gaps are listed at the end of the page, because they are where your own experience will carry the piece.

One shape for all five

The same running order works for each piece, in the style of the preemptive negative keywords tool page:

  • The subject. What it is, in plain words, and one myth to clear up early.
  • Why it matters. What goes wrong, in money, when nobody looks.
  • What it covers. The parts to check.
  • How to act on it. What to change, in what order, and how to tell whether it worked.
  • Build your own. The Google Ads API fields or the CSV export that gets you the same data.
  • By the way. "I built a free tool that does this for you."

One idea runs through all five and could be a series line: the account can look fine on average while something specific is broken. Settings drift, a link dies, one keyword drags the Quality Score down, one network wastes the budget, one page loads slowly on a phone. Each tool looks underneath the average.

1. Settings audit

Most accounts aren't badly managed. They're badly set up, often by someone who left years ago, and nobody has looked at the settings since.

What it is

  • A settings audit checks the configuration you inherited before you change anything. Use click-through rate, conversion rate and impression share to decide where to look first. Then check targets, network settings and auto-applied recommendations, and write down which changes you keep. Practitioner
  • It is mostly about what Smart Bidding is actually aiming at: which conversion action, its goal category, the account default goals and any campaign-level override. Practitioner
  • Settings sit at more than one level. Tracking templates at account, campaign and ad group level can overwrite or duplicate each other, and a campaign setting overrides the account one. Practitioner

Why it matters

  • Settings alone can move reported results while the business stays flat. A change to a lead's static value, a second primary action or a counting setting can lift reported ROAS with no extra revenue. Practitioner
  • Accounts change without anyone noticing. Auto-applied recommendations can broaden keywords, add Display expansion, switch bidding or raise budgets. Third-party tools can edit bids, keywords or tracking templates too. Change history is where it shows. Practitioner
  • Search CTR and conversion rate that look too good, with few real enquiries behind them, often trace back to a network setting or a faulty conversion event. Practitioner
  • Settings can stop ads showing at all. Google's own list of reasons includes status, dates, targeting, bids, budget, policy limits, Ad Rank and conversion tracking problems. Google

What it covers

Conversion tracking and counting. The one to spend most time on, because bidding learns from it.

  • Primary actions count in the Conversions column and can be bid on. Secondary actions show in All conversions only. Google
  • A secondary action can still be bid on if it is in a custom goal the campaign uses. Good myth to bust. Google
  • Google recommends counting "Every" for sales and "One" for leads, set per conversion action. "One" counts once per click per action. It does not remove duplicates across actions, so two primary lead actions can still double count. Google
  • When several goals track stages of one funnel, Google recommends only one stage as the account default goal. Google
  • Leads should fire on a confirmed submission or booking. Purchases should carry a transaction ID, value and currency that match the order, with one primary action counting them. Practitioner
  • Enhanced conversions for web and for leads are now one setting, with an opt-out per action. Google
  • Auto-tagging: worth a line on what it does (it adds the click ID, gclid, that ties a visit to a click) and why it should be on. Outside

Bidding and targets.

  • Maximise conversions aims at the number of conversions, Maximise conversion value at the recorded value. Without a target, both try to spend the budget. Google
  • A CPA or ROAS target adds an efficiency aim that can hold back delivery. Check the quality and volume of the conversions first. Practitioner
  • Google cites at least 30 conversions (50 for Target ROAS) in the period it recommends for judging a strategy. Google
  • Since August 2026, budget-limited Target CPA and Target ROAS campaigns bid closer to their target instead of beating it. Google won't change the target or budget for you, and a tighter target will likely cut daily spend. Topical, worth a mention. Google

Location targeting. A classic, easy win for the piece.

  • "Presence or interest" can include people who are only interested in a place. "Presence" keeps it to people likely to be in, or regularly in, the area. Google
  • To keep Search ads to a service area, choose Presence. Location is estimated, so check the location report for leakage. Google
  • The right choice depends on the campaign type. Google recommends Presence or interest for Demand Gen. Google

Networks.

  • Split Google Search, Search Partners and any Display traffic by spend and real results before deciding to keep or drop them. Practitioner
  • Display Network ticked on a Search campaign is one of the settings that is almost always a mistake. Outside

Budgets.

  • For most campaigns, Google can spend up to twice the average daily budget in a day, and up to 30.4 times it in a month. A day over budget is normal, not a bug. Google
  • "Limited by budget" means the budget is below what Google thinks it could spend. "Limited by bid strategy" points to the target instead. Google

AI Max and Performance Max. The newest settings and the ones people know least.

  • AI Max has three parts: search term matching, text customisation and Final URL expansion. Final URL expansion needs text customisation, so turning one off turns off both. Google
  • Final URL expansion can override pinned ad copy. To keep landing pages to an approved list, use ad group URL inclusions with expansion off. Google
  • In Performance Max, Final URL expansion lets Google pick other pages on your domain. URL exclusions limit it, and asset reporting shows what served. Google
  • Google recommends brand exclusions, not negative keywords, for unwanted brand searches in Performance Max. Its negative keywords cover Search and Shopping only. Google

Access. Review who can reach the account once a year: remove or downgrade users, check agency access through manager accounts, use passkeys. Practitioner

How to act on it

  • Review each auto-applied recommendation against what the account is for. Many practitioners switch them all off and make the changes by hand. Practitioner
  • Test conversion tracking end to end: submit a real form, check it reaches the CRM, and check the event and how often it fires in Tag Assistant or GTM preview. Practitioner
  • Bring in new CRM lead imports as secondary first. Make them primary once they arrive reliably. Practitioner
  • Change one thing at a time and give it one or two conversion cycles. Raising the budget and tightening the target on the same day makes the result impossible to read. Google Practitioner
  • Write down the starting state, the reason, the number you expect to move and a date to look again. Practitioner
  • Then keep watching: alerts on edits by unknown users or tools, and a daily check of conversions against the same weekday in recent weeks. Practitioner
  • Your own angle could be: there is no single right setting. Decide your standards once (Presence only, no Display on Search, auto-apply off) and check every account against them. That is exactly how the tool works. Outside

Myths to clear up

  • "One" counting merges all of a customer's conversions. It doesn't.
  • Secondary actions never affect bidding. A custom goal can make them biddable.
  • A ROAS jump means growth. Check value, counting and primary actions first.
  • Pinned headlines are always honoured. Not with AI Max's Final URL expansion on.

Build your own

  • One GAQL query per resource covers most of it. customer: auto_tagging_enabled, tracking_url_template, final_url_suffix. campaign: network_settings.* (Search Partners and Display), geo_target_type_setting.positive_geo_target_type (Presence or Presence or interest), bidding_strategy_type, budget. conversion_action: counting_type, primary_for_goal, status, type. Auto-apply settings are in recommendation_subscription. Outside
  • Without the API: the campaign table can show columns for networks, location options and bid strategy, and downloads as a CSV. Google Ads Editor exports every setting of every campaign in one file, which is the easiest no-code route. Outside

By the way: the tool

Shabba's Settings audit reads the account and every enabled campaign, and groups each setting by value, so you see "12 campaigns on Presence, 3 on Presence or interest" and which ones. It holds no opinion of its own. You turn on your standards (nine start on: auto-tagging, auto-apply, conversion tracking, lead counting, one primary action per category, Universal Analytics imports, serving status, location option, Display on Search) and it shows what doesn't meet them. 2. Broken links

Google Ads will keep charging you for clicks to a page that doesn't exist. Nothing in the account stops that unless you build the check yourself.

What it is

  • Every ad, keyword, sitelink and Performance Max asset group can carry its own final URL. Over time pages get moved, deleted or renamed, and the ads keep pointing at the old address. Outside
  • The URLs you typed aren't the only ones in use. With Final URL expansion, Performance Max can send people to other pages on your domain. Google
  • Tracking templates can be set at three levels. Lower levels override higher ones and parameters can be duplicated, so the address a visitor lands on can differ from the one you set. Practitioner
  • In Shopping, a product page Google can't reach shows in Merchant Center as a "Product page unavailable" disapproval. Practitioner

Why it matters

  • If the site goes down, the ads keep running and you pay for visitors who land on an error page. Practitioner
  • A broken page looks like a performance problem. When conversions fall, check the site, the conversion path and recent site changes before touching bids or copy. Good hook: people rewrite ads to fix a 404. Practitioner
  • Broken tracking mostly damages measurement. Duplicate UTMs make analytics disagree with Google Ads. Practitioner
  • Google's destination requirements disapprove ads whose landing page doesn't work, but only once Google finds it, and a sitelink or a low-traffic page can sit broken for months. Outside

What it covers

  • Status codes, in plain words: 404 (not found) and 410 (gone) mean the page has gone. 5xx means the server failed. A 301 or 302 redirect works but is slower and may land somewhere less relevant. Outside
  • The false alarms: 401, 403 and 429 usually mean the site turned the checker away, not that the page is broken. A time-out can be a blip. Check twice, a few minutes apart, before calling a page broken. (This is how the tool decides.) Outside
  • Pages Performance Max chose itself, not only the ones you entered. Google
  • Shopping product pages, where Merchant Center checks that the feed's price and availability match the page. Google
  • The whole journey after the click: page, form or call, and whether the lead reaches the CRM. A working page with a broken form is still a broken link in all but name. Practitioner

How to act on it

  • Check by spend. A broken page that got 2,000 clicks last month matters more than ten that got none. Outside
  • Fix it everywhere at once. The same old URL often sits in ads, sitelinks and keywords across several campaigns. Outside
  • Ask the site owner for a redirect from the old address as well, so organic links and old emails keep working too. Outside
  • Schedule a site check that pauses campaigns when the site is down, sends an alert, and turns them back on when it recovers. Test it on one campaign first, and read any copied script before you run it. Practitioner
  • Set a daily conversion-drop alert against the same weekday in recent weeks. When it fires, check tracking, budget, the site and stock. Practitioner
  • In Performance Max, look at expanded URLs in asset reporting and exclude pages you don't want used. Google
  • For "Product page unavailable", request a site check in Merchant Center for products that sell, and open the page yourself. Practitioner
  • If another team owns the site, send them the evidence and one specific fix. Practitioner

Myths to clear up

  • "Google would tell me if a page broke." Only sometimes, and not quickly.
  • "My URLs are the ones in my ads." Not with Final URL expansion on.
  • "Every disapproval is the site's fault." Check the page directly first.

Build your own

  • Collect the URLs: ad_group_ad.ad.final_urls, ad_group_criterion.final_urls (keyword-level URLs), asset.final_urls for sitelinks, and asset_group.final_urls for Performance Max. landing_page_view and expanded_landing_page_view list the pages that actually got clicks, with cost, which is the easy way to rank them. Outside
  • Request each one with a normal GET (some servers answer HEAD wrongly), follow redirects, and record the final status. Retry errors once a few minutes later. Outside
  • Without the API: the Landing pages report downloads as a CSV. Paste the URLs into any crawler or a simple script. A Google Ads script can do the whole thing inside the account on a schedule. Outside

By the way: the tool

Shabba's Broken link checker lists every URL in your ads, keywords, sitelinks and asset groups, highest spend first, across one or more accounts, and checks each one. An error is checked again a few minutes later, and a site turning the checker away (401, 403, 429) doesn't count. Fix swaps a broken or redirecting URL for a new one everywhere the account uses it, through the change queue. Each link has 13 months of history, and a schedule can alert you when a page that had impressions yesterday stops working.

3. Quality Score

Quality Score is the most quoted number in Google Ads and one of the most misread. It's a diagnosis, not a target.

What it is

  • A keyword-level diagnostic, from 1 to 10, built from three parts: expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Each part is rated Above average, Average or Below average. A keyword without enough data shows no score. Google Outside
  • Google says the number you see isn't used in the auction. It's a reading of how keyword, ad and page are doing, not a lever. Strong opening myth. Google
  • Expected CTR is a forecast, not your actual CTR. A keyword can have a healthy CTR and still show Below average. Practitioner
  • Ad Strength is a different thing: a rating of your responsive search ad's variety. It doesn't decide Ad Rank. People mix the two up all the time. Google

Why it matters

  • Its main use is triage. The three ratings tell you whether to work on the ad, the keyword grouping or the landing page first. Practitioner
  • The same things that make the score better feed Ad Rank, which decides whether and where the ad shows. Google's diagnostics name "insufficient Ad Rank" as a reason ads don't serve. Google
  • To measure what weak quality costs you, use Search lost impression share (rank). It separates impressions lost to Ad Rank from those lost to budget. Google
  • When cost per lead jumps but conversion rate holds, check whether CPC rose first, and split it by query, campaign and network before blaming the market. Practitioner

What it covers

  • Expected CTR: how likely your ad is to be clicked for that keyword. Practitioner
  • Ad relevance: how closely the ad matches the intent behind the keyword. Tied to grouping keywords by shared buyer intent, so one ad can make one clear promise. Practitioner
  • Landing page experience: whether the page delivers what the ad promised and works well for the visitor. A natural bridge to the page speed piece. Google Practitioner

How to read it across an account

This is the section the knowledge base can't give you, and where the tool earns its place. Outside

  • A simple average of keyword scores is misleading, because a keyword with ten impressions counts the same as one with ten thousand. Weight by impressions (or by spend) instead.
  • Look at the share of spend on each score, not the count of keywords. "40% of spend is on keywords scoring 3 or below" is the sentence that gets action.
  • Watch it over time. A score that falls after a site change or a new ad tells you more than the score on its own.

An illustration with made-up numbers:

Keyword QS Impressions A 3 9,000 B 9 1,000 Simple average: (3 + 9) / 2 = 6 Weighted: (3 × 9,000 + 9 × 1,000) / 10,000 = 3.6

Same account, two very different stories.

How to act on it

  • Start with the keywords carrying most impressions, not the lowest scores. Practitioner
  • Look at all three ratings together. A Below average expected CTR can really be a relevance or page problem. Practitioner
  • Expected CTR: see what competitors say in Auction Insights and the Ads Transparency Center, then write a challenger ad. Specific promises help: a time frame, a type of buyer, a price. Judge it on conversion rate and lead quality as well as CTR. Practitioner
  • Ad relevance: group keywords by intent so each ad group's ad speaks to it. Pin only when you must, because pinning cuts the combinations Google can try. Practitioner Google
  • Landing page experience: follow the promise from query to ad to page. If different buyers need different proof, give them different pages. Load the top pages on a phone as a first-time visitor and time them. Practitioner

Myths to clear up

  • "Switch broad to exact and the score goes up." Google says match type doesn't change how the score is calculated. Google
  • "Quality Score is what I bid with." The displayed number isn't an auction input.
  • "Below average expected CTR means nobody clicks." It's a forecast.
  • "Higher CTR is always better." A high-CTR ad can bring worse leads. Practitioner
  • "Good Ad Strength means good Quality Score." Separate ratings.

Build your own

  • From keyword_view: ad_group_criterion.quality_info.quality_score, .creative_quality_score (ad relevance), .post_click_quality_score (landing page) and .search_predicted_ctr (expected CTR), plus metrics.impressions and metrics.cost_micros to weight them. Outside
  • For the history, metrics.historical_quality_score and its three component siblings, segmented by segments.date or segments.week. Outside
  • Without the API: add the Quality Score columns (and the "(hist.)" versions) to the keywords table, download it as a CSV, and do the weighting in a spreadsheet with SUMPRODUCT. Outside

By the way: the tool

Shabba's Quality Score dashboard shows, for one account and a date range, how spend splits across scores 1 to 10 (plus spend Google hasn't scored), how it splits across the three ratings, the impression-weighted account score for each of the last 12 weeks, and each campaign's score. Read only. Exports to CSV.

4. Account breakdowns

Averages lie. An account can look healthy overall while half its spend is going somewhere that never converts.

What it is

  • Splitting spend and results by one thing at a time: match type, brand or non-brand, campaign type, network, device, hour and day of week, location. Outside
  • When you split Search by network (Google Search, Search Partners, Display), you see spend and real results for each. The blended figure hides the difference. Practitioner
  • Brand against non-brand is a split by query, not by campaign name. Sort the searches a campaign actually matched into brand, competitor and non-brand, typos included. Practitioner

Why it matters

  • A strong average can be carried by one segment while another wastes money. The knowledge base makes this point for networks and brand. A simple made-up example makes it land: Practitioner
Network Clicks Conversions Conv. rate Google Search 1,000 50 5.0% Search Partners 500 5 1.0% Blended 1,500 55 3.7%
  • "3.7%" describes neither network. Outside
  • A non-brand campaign can look strong because brand searches leak into it. Measure brand's share inside it before you trust it. Practitioner
  • Performance Max's return can be propped up by brand searches. Check the brand share before judging it. Practitioner
  • More brand activity can be demand you already had. Compare with new-customer results and total sales. Practitioner
  • When CTR and conversion rate look implausibly high with few real enquiries, a network split shows which network made the clicks and the "conversions". Practitioner

What it covers

  • Match type. Exact and phrase reach close variants, so check the searches they really trigger, not the label. Google Practitioner
  • Brand and non-brand. Across Search, Shopping and Performance Max. Practitioner
  • Campaign type and channel. Search, Shopping, Performance Max, Demand Gen, and the network each click came from. For Performance Max, use the channel performance report for results by channel. Google says the placement report is for brand safety, not profitability. Google
  • Device. When conversion rate drops but CPC holds, check the mobile and desktop conversion paths and any shift in the traffic mix. Practitioner
  • Hour and day. The knowledge base only covers using the same weekday as the baseline for monitoring. The rest is yours. Practitioner
  • Location. Check the location report for leakage outside the target area. Google

How to act on it

  • Networks: decide on Search Partners or Display only after comparing each one's spend and real results. Practitioner
  • Brand: in Performance Max, use brand exclusions for unwanted brand searches. There's an option to keep Shopping ads on brand searches while excluding text ads. A brand negative list on non-brand campaigns is the common practitioner route. Check the brand campaign can handle its own demand once you split it out. Google Practitioner
  • Structure: split campaigns when budget, geography or economics need separate control. With a small budget, combine similar campaigns instead, so data isn't thinned. Practitioner
  • Location: if the report shows leakage, consider Presence. Google
  • Waste over time: track cost per conversion across all searches against cost per conversion for converting searches. A rising ratio points to waste. Practitioner
  • Device, hour and day: with Smart Bidding these splits are mostly for understanding, not for bid adjustments. Worth your own view here, since the knowledge base is silent. Outside

Myths and mistakes

  • Comparing recent days with older ones while conversions are still arriving. Recent dates always look weak. Google suggests reviewing days-to-conversion on a range that ended at least 30 days ago. Google
  • Splitting a small segment and reading a lot into five conversions.
  • Judging a segment on a conversion that doesn't match real enquiries. Reconcile with the CRM first. Practitioner
  • Treating attributed brand conversions as growth. Practitioner
  • Reading Demand Gen channel by channel. Google suggests judging it on the whole campaign. Google

Build your own

  • Mostly one field each, added to a campaign query with cost, clicks, conversions and value: segments.ad_network_type, segments.device, segments.hour, segments.day_of_week, and campaign.advertising_channel_type for campaign type. Match type comes from keyword_view with ad_group_criterion.keyword.match_type. Brand needs your own list of brand words matched against search_term_view. Location from user_location_view. Outside
  • Without the API: the Segment button in the campaigns table does most of these, and the predefined reports (time, device, network) download as CSVs. Brand against non-brand needs the search terms report and a filter on your brand words. Outside

By the way: the tool

Shabba's Account breakdowns shows one account's spend split seven ways in one run: keyword spend by match type, brand and non-brand, campaign type, channel, device, hour of day and day of week. Each split shows every segment's share, spend, clicks, conversions and value. Read only. Exports to CSV.

5. Landing page speed

You can have the best ad in the auction and still lose the sale while the page loads. Bids and copy can't fix a slow page.

What it is

  • Landing page experience is one of Quality Score's three parts. Speed is one part of that experience, alongside a clear offer and a working form or checkout. Google Practitioner
  • Two kinds of measurement, worth explaining simply. Lab data is one test run now, on a simulated phone, which is good for finding causes. Field data is what real Chrome visitors experienced over the last 28 days, which is good for knowing the truth, but only exists for pages with enough visitors. Outside
  • Core Web Vitals, Google's three headline measures: LCP (how long until the main content shows, good at 2.5 seconds or less), INP (how quickly the page responds to a tap, good at 200 milliseconds or less, it replaced FID in 2024) and CLS (how much the page jumps around as it loads, good at 0.1 or less). Google judges them at the 75th percentile of visits. Outside

Why it matters

  • Slow pages and checkout friction lower conversion rate whatever the ad says. Practitioner
  • Cost per lead is roughly CPC divided by conversion rate, so a slower page raises the cost of every lead directly. A neat way to put speed in money terms. Practitioner
  • Be careful with "a faster page raises your Quality Score and cuts your CPC". The displayed score isn't an auction input. Speed pays through conversion rate first. Google

What it covers

  • Mobile and desktop separately. Try the path as a new customer on both. Practitioner
  • The whole journey, not just the first load: form, call, checkout and the CRM receiving the lead. Practitioner
  • The pages that actually get the traffic. With Final URL expansion, Performance Max may be sending people to pages you never chose. Check expanded URLs in asset reporting. Google
  • The usual causes, for a short list in the piece: large images, too many third-party tags and chat widgets, slow server response, web fonts, and banners or cookie notices that push the page down. Outside

How to act on it

  • Start with the top landing pages by spend. Load them on a phone as a first-time visitor and time them, before changing bids or copy. Practitioner
  • Measure each fix: speed and completion rate before and after, plus the starting state, reason, expected change and a date to look again. Practitioner
  • If you can, test the faster page against the current one at the same time, one change at a time, allowing for late conversions. Practitioner
  • The ads team often doesn't own the site. Write down the slow steps with evidence and give the site owner one specific change. Practitioner
  • Keep checking. A new tag or plugin can undo the work overnight, so compare each test with the last one. Outside
  • Check that tracking still fires after any site change. A tag migration can double count or drop conversions, and get blamed on the speed fix. Practitioner

Myths to clear up

  • "Fix speed and Quality Score jumps." Speed is one input to one of three parts.
  • "It's fast on my laptop." Test on a phone, as a new visitor. Repeat buyers hide problems new mobile visitors hit. Practitioner
  • "A score of 100 is the goal." The score is a lab summary. Real-visitor timings and conversion rate are what matter. Outside

Build your own

  • Get the pages and their spend from landing_page_view (or expanded_landing_page_view) with metrics.cost_micros, sorted by cost. Outside
  • Run each through the free PageSpeed Insights API (pagespeedonline/v5/runPagespeed, with strategy=mobile and category=performance). It returns the lab score and timings, and real-visitor data when Google has enough. The Chrome UX Report API gives the real-visitor history. A free API key raises the request limit. Outside
  • Without the API: download the Landing pages report as a CSV and paste the top pages into pagespeed.web.dev one by one. Fine for ten pages, tedious for fifty. Outside

Where you'll need your own experience

The knowledge base had little or nothing on these. They're where your own view, or a fresh look at Google's help pages, will carry the piece.

  • Settings audit: auto-tagging and gclid, GA4 goal imports against the Google Ads tag, ad schedules and device settings, tracking template mechanics (final URL suffix, ValueTrack, parallel tracking), attribution and conversion windows, Performance Max audience signals and customer acquisition goal, and an order and cadence for the audit.
  • Broken links: the Destination not working policy itself (what triggers it, how fast, how to appeal), soft 404s, redirect chains and domain rules, how a broken sitelink is treated against a broken ad, and pages removed from the site after the ads were built.
  • Quality Score: the 1 to 10 scale and its ratings, how quality feeds Ad Rank and actual CPC, how often scores update, weighting by spend, and where Quality Score doesn't apply (Performance Max, Shopping).
  • Account breakdowns: device, schedule and location bid adjustments and how Smart Bidding treats them, how to read hour-of-day data, how much data a segment needs before you act on it, and the Search Partners and Display checkboxes in Search campaigns.
  • Page speed: PageSpeed Insights, lab against field data, Core Web Vitals and their thresholds, Google's own definition of landing page experience, published studies on speed and conversion, and specific fixes.

Worth adding to the knowledge base later, so the next round (and the Second Opinion notes on the finished pieces) has more to go on.
